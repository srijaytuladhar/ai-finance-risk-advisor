# Financial Risk and Portfolio Management Glossary

Understanding portfolio risk metrics is essential for building and maintaining a resilient investment strategy. Below are plain-English explanations of key quantitative risk metrics and portfolio management concepts used by financial advisors and institutional asset managers.

## 1. Value at Risk (VaR)
Value at Risk (VaR) measures the maximum expected dollar loss or percentage loss of an investment portfolio over a specified time horizon at a given statistical confidence level. For example, a 1-day 95% confidence VaR of 2.5% means that on 95 out of 100 normal trading days, the portfolio is expected to lose no more than 2.5% of its total value. Conversely, there is a 5% chance that the loss on any given day will exceed 2.5%. VaR can be calculated using historical simulation, parametric normal distribution models, or Monte Carlo simulations. In this platform, historical simulation evaluates actual past return distributions without imposing artificial normal distribution assumptions on market tail events.

## 2. Sharpe Ratio
The Sharpe ratio measures the excess return generated by an investment portfolio per unit of total risk (volatility), relative to a benchmark risk-free rate (such as US Treasury yields). 
The formula is:
Sharpe Ratio = (Portfolio Return - Risk-Free Rate) / Portfolio Standard Deviation

A higher Sharpe ratio indicates better risk-adjusted performance. As a rule of thumb:
- Less than 1.0: Sub-optimal risk-adjusted return
- 1.0 to 1.99: Good risk-adjusted return
- 2.0 or higher: Excellent risk-adjusted return
By accounting for volatility, the Sharpe ratio prevents investors from confusing high absolute returns caused by reckless risk-taking with genuine investment alpha.

## 3. Maximum Drawdown (MDD)
Maximum Drawdown represents the peak-to-trough decline of a portfolio before a new peak is reached over a specific observation period. It measures the worst-case cumulative loss an investor would have suffered if they bought at the highest price peak and held through the lowest trough. For instance, a maximum drawdown of -18.4% indicates that the portfolio plunged 18.4% from its historical high water mark before recovering. Maximum drawdown is a vital measure of downside risk, financial pain threshold, and capital preservation.

## 4. Volatility
Volatility measures the degree of variation in an asset or portfolio's trading prices over time, typically quantified by the annualized standard deviation of daily percentage returns. High volatility means prices swing sharply up or down within short time spans, whereas low volatility denotes more stable, predictable price trajectories. Annualized volatility is derived by multiplying daily standard deviation by the square root of 252 (the approximate number of trading days in a calendar year).

## 5. Beta
Beta measures a portfolio's sensitivity and systematic risk relative to a broader market benchmark, traditionally the S&P 500 index (ticker: SPY).
- Beta = 1.0: The portfolio moves in lockstep with the market.
- Beta > 1.0: The portfolio is more volatile than the market (e.g., Beta 1.25 means the portfolio tends to move 25% more than the market).
- Beta < 1.0: The portfolio is less volatile and more defensive than the market.
- Negative Beta: The portfolio tends to move inversely to the market.

## 6. Portfolio Rebalancing
Portfolio rebalancing is the disciplined process of realigning the weightings of assets in an investment portfolio back to their targeted asset allocation. Over time, high-performing securities grow into an oversized percentage of the portfolio, concentrating risk in specific sectors or companies. Rebalancing systematically sells a portion of over-weighted outperforming assets and buys under-weighted lagging assets, enforcing a prudent "buy low, sell high" regimen while ensuring the portfolio stays aligned with the investor's intended risk tolerance and investment mandate.
